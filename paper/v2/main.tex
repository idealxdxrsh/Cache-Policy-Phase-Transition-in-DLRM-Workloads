\begin{abstract}
Embedding lookups take a large share of deep-learning recommendation model (DLRM) inference time and are skewed.
On one H100, the L2 read hit rate of the embedding kernels on the Criteo stream falls from \R{p14.pt.64}\% to \R{p14.pt.16384}\% as the batch grows from \R{p14.B.64} to
\R{p14.B.16384} (one kernel per table). We study LFU replacement for a small embedding cache, from traces to layout. A hardware LFU controller breaks count ties by the lowest slot index; its exact
trace model loses to LRU in a cache shared by all \R{ds.tables} tables at all four sizes simulated from \R{sz.256}
to \R{sz.100000} entries (\R{p9.10M.256.v2}\% against \R{p3b.10M.256.LRU}\% at \R{sz.256} entries, first
\R{lab.10M} Criteo rows), and an ablation assigns the whole gap to the tie-break. Rotate-victim, a one-pointer
change, reproduces LFU with oldest-first ties (\R{p11.10M.256.v3}\% at \R{sz.256} entries) and beats LRU and the
old rule at all six simulated sizes, \R{sz.16} to \R{sz.100000} entries; its RTL matches the model request by
request on test slices. With an STMicroelectronics 28nm FD-SOI library at the typical corner, both versions meet a
\R{p15.met} ns clock and fail \R{p15.fail} ns after routing at \R{sz.256} entries, and in post-route
simulation at \R{p15.gls.period} ns the new rule needs \R{p15.dE.row1M.abs} to \R{p15.dE.row0.abs}\% less energy
per request, as it needs fewer cycles under the testbench's fill model. Code and aggregate results are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A deep-learning recommendation model (DLRM) maps each categorical feature of a request to a row of an embedding
table and combines the pooled rows with dense features in multilayer perceptrons~\cite{naumov2019dlrm}. The lookups
are irregular, memory-bound and take a large share of inference time~\cite{gupta2020architectural,ke2020recnmp}.
Their accesses are skewed, and several systems keep the frequently used rows in a faster memory: a DRAM cache in
front of non-volatile memory~\cite{eisenman2019bandana}, hot embeddings in GPU memory~\cite{adnan2022fae}, or a
GPU-side cache that looks ahead in the access stream~\cite{kwon2022scratchpipe}. Each needs a replacement rule.

How well does a general-purpose cache serve these lookups? We profiled PyTorch's \texttt{nn.EmbeddingBag}~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} on the Criteo
stream on one H100 with hardware counters (Fig.~\ref{fig:l2}). The L2 read hit rate of the embedding kernels falls
from \R{p14.pt.64}\% at batch \R{p14.B.64} to \R{p14.pt.1024}\% at \R{p14.B.1024} and \R{p14.pt.16384}\% at
\R{p14.B.16384} with one kernel per table, and from \R{p14.cc.64}\% to \R{p14.cc.16384}\% with all tables
concatenated. The profiled window starts at the same row for every batch size, so it grows with the batch: batch
size and window length change together. In our trace simulator, one cache of \R{p14.cap} vectors shared by all tables, the simulated capacity
closest to the \R{p14.l2bytes}-byte L2 at \R{p14.vecbytes} bytes per vector, reaches \R{p3b.2M.100000.LRU}\% with LRU
and \R{p3b.2M.100000.LFUr}\% with LFU-reset over the first \R{lab.2M} rows. The comparison is indicative, not a validation:
the L2 has its own replacement policy, works on \R{p14.sectorbytes}-byte sectors and also holds indices, offsets and
outputs, and the counters cover a \R{p14.batches}-batch window, whereas the simulator counts vector hits over the
whole trace from a cold cache.

A frequency-aware cache next to the lookup datapath needs a small replacement unit. We take a simple hardware LFU
controller (flip-flop storage, saturating counters, a two-stage minimum search) and ask whether it reaches the hit
rate of the LFU policy it was written to implement, and at what cost in a commercial process. Contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item A trace-driven comparison of replacement policies on Criteo (seven per table, five in a shared cache) at two
trace lengths (Sections~\ref{sec:method}, \ref{sec:res-policy}).
\item The finding that the controller's lowest-slot tie-break makes its exact model lose to LRU in a shared cache
at all four sizes simulated from \R{sz.256} to \R{sz.100000} entries, with an ablation that assigns the whole gap to the tie-break
(Section~\ref{sec:tie}).
\item Rotate-victim, a fix with one pointer register that reproduces LFU with oldest-first ties, verified request
by request against the simulator on test slices in RTL simulation and cycle by cycle against the RTL at gate level
(Sections~\ref{sec:fix}, \ref{sec:verif}).
\item Synthesis, place and route and multi-corner timing of both versions with a commercial 28nm FD-SOI library:
v2 and v3 meet and fail the same clock periods before routing (\R{p12.br.256.met} and \R{p12.br.256.fail} ns) and
after it (\R{p15.met} and \R{p15.fail} ns), and v3 needs \R{p15.dE.row1M.abs} to \R{p15.dE.row0.abs}\% less energy
per request in post-route simulation (Section~\ref{sec:res-hw}).
\item Public RTL, simulators, flow scripts and aggregate results.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:bg}
\textbf{Embedding tables.} In the Criteo display-advertising data~\cite{criteo2014dac} each of \R{ds.tables}
categorical columns holds hashed values; with one table per column, each distinct value is one embedding vector.
Production characterisations show that embedding operators can dominate inference time~\cite{gupta2020architectural},
and the accesses are skewed, so that a small set of hot rows receives many of the lookups~\cite{eisenman2019bandana,adnan2022fae}.

\textbf{Replacement policies.} LRU evicts the least recently used entry. LFU evicts the entry with the fewest
accesses; variants differ in what happens to a count on eviction. \emph{LFU-reset} discards the count and restarts
at one on re-insertion; \emph{LFU-persistent} keeps a count for every address ever seen, an idealised model of
history-keeping LFU. TinyLFU adds an admission filter that compares the estimated frequency of the new item with
that of the eviction candidate, and W-TinyLFU puts a small LRU window in front of the main
cache~\cite{einziger2017tinylfu}, estimating frequencies with a Count-Min sketch~\cite{cormode2005countmin}. ARC
balances a recency list and a frequency list using ghost lists of evicted keys~\cite{megiddo2003arc}. Belady's MIN
evicts the entry used furthest in the future and gives an offline upper bound for a cache that fetches on
demand~\cite{belady1966study}.

\textbf{Caches for recommendation.} Bandana places embeddings in non-volatile memory behind a DRAM
cache~\cite{eisenman2019bandana}; FAE keeps hot embeddings in GPU
memory~\cite{adnan2022fae}; ScratchPipe builds a GPU-side cache that uses future accesses known from the training
pipeline~\cite{kwon2022scratchpipe}; RecNMP performs embedding operations near memory~\cite{ke2020recnmp}. We study a lower level: the replacement
decision inside one small hardware cache, where the tie-break between equal counts is a design choice of the circuit.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Workload.} We use the Criteo Display Advertising Challenge training file (\R{ds.rows} rows) and replay its
first \R{ds.r2M} and \R{ds.r10M} rows (\R{lab.2M} and \R{lab.10M} below); nothing is claimed for the rest of the file.
Empty fields produce no access. \emph{Per-table} caches give each table its own cache of $C$ vectors ($C$ from
\R{sz.p3min} to \R{sz.100000}). A \emph{shared} cache holds vectors of all tables under the key (table, value), with
the accesses of one row issued in table order; this order is a modelling choice. The shared stream has
\R{p11.acc.2M} accesses at \R{lab.2M} rows and \R{p11.acc.10M} at \R{lab.10M}. Capacities are in vectors.

\textbf{Policies.} LRU, LFU-reset, LFU-persistent, TinyLFU with exact counters, W-TinyLFU with a Count-Min sketch
(both with a \R{p3.window}\% window), ARC and Belady. In the simulator, LFU-reset breaks count ties in favour of the
entry installed earliest (\emph{oldest-first}) and saturates its counters at \R{rtl.simcap}. The simulators have
unit tests and mutation tests that check that the tests fail on deliberately broken policies.

\textbf{Controller, v2.} The controller (tag \texttt{rtl-v2-final}, Fig.~\ref{fig:rtl}) stores tags,
\R{rtl.vecw}-bit data words, counters and valid bits in flip-flops, with no SRAM macro. Parameters: address width
\R{rtl.addrw}, counter width \R{rtl.cntw} (saturating at \R{rtl.sat}), \R{rtl.groups} groups, depth \R{sz.16},
\R{sz.64} or \R{sz.256} lines. The tag compare runs per group on registered copies of the request address and a
priority tree gives the hit index. On a miss, Stage~A (a minimum tree per group, registered in LOOKUP) and Stage~B
(across groups, in EVICT) find the line with the smallest key $\mathit{eff}$, which is zero for an invalid line and
the count plus one otherwise. With \texttt{COUNTER\_MODE} 0 (default) an installed tag starts at count one, so the
controller belongs to the LFU-reset family; with mode 1 the tag continues from the count left in the slot. Both trees
keep the left operand on equal keys, so the victim is the \emph{lowest slot index} among the minimum counts.

\textbf{Controller, v3 (Rotate-victim).} v3 adds a pointer register \texttt{ptr} that loads victim${}+1$
(modulo the depth) when a fill completes, and extends the tree key by one wrap bit, set when the slot index is below
\texttt{ptr} (key width \R{p10.kw} bits). The victim is then the first minimum-count slot at or after \texttt{ptr},
wrapping around. Tree depth, counters and FSM are unchanged; mode 1 keeps the v2 rule. In mode 0 the flip-flop count
grows by the pointer width (\R{p11.ff.16}, \R{p11.ff.64} and \R{p11.ff.256} at the three depths).

\textbf{RTL-exact simulator.} To compare the controller with the policies on full traces, we wrote a trace-simulator
policy that models the RTL exactly: slots, lowest-index filling of invalid lines, the key $\mathit{eff}$, the
lowest-slot victim, the install count of each mode and saturation at \R{rtl.sat}. Against the RTL hit flag of
\R{p9.runs} zero-delay RTL simulations (three depths, both modes, two cold-start slices of \R{p9.slicereq}
consecutive requests of the shared stream, and their prefixes), it gives \R{p9.mism} mismatches in
\R{p9.req} requests. For the ablation we built three variants of LFU-reset: lowest-slot ties, counters saturating at
\R{rtl.sat}, and both together; the last equals the RTL-exact policy in mode 0.

\textbf{Verification.} The RTL is checked by a self-checking testbench (\R{rtl.tests} tests, plus a tie-order test
for v3), by mutation testing (the tests must fail on broken copies of the RTL), by lockstep comparison with the
simulator or with earlier RTL versions, by Yosys equivalence checking, and by gate-level simulation in lockstep with
the RTL.

\textbf{Implementation flows.} Table~\ref{tab:flows} lists the flows. The open flow maps the RTL to the NanGate45
library~\cite{nangate45} with Yosys and ABC~\cite{wolf_yosys,brayton2010abc} and runs OpenSTA~\cite{opensta}
before layout. The commercial flow uses an STMicroelectronics 28nm FD-SOI standard-cell library (accessed through
CMP) with Cadence Genus for synthesis, Xcelium for gate-level simulation, Genus for power with simulated switching
activity, and Innovus for place and route; area is in library units. The floorplan has end caps, well taps and a
four-net power grid (supply, ground and the two body-bias nets of FD-SOI, which connect to the well taps). Each
design point was run once.

\textbf{Per-table and shared caches.} With per-table caches both LFU variants beat LRU at \R{sz.256} vectors
(Table~\ref{tab:hit}) and at every other capacity of the sweep from \R{sz.p3min} to \R{sz.100000} vectors, at both
trace lengths. With one shared cache the tables
compete for the same entries and the two LFU variants part ways (Fig.~\ref{fig:shared}). LFU-reset beats LRU at every shared size tested
(\R{sz.256} to \R{sz.100000}) at both lengths. LFU-persistent does not: it is below LRU at \R{sz.256} entries at
both lengths and at \R{sz.1000} entries at \R{lab.10M} rows (\R{p3b.10M.1000.LFUp}\% against
\R{p3b.10M.1000.LRU}\%), and it loses the most from the shorter to the longer trace, \R{p3b.drop.lo} to
\R{p3b.drop.hi} percentage points at \R{sz.256} to \R{sz.10000} entries. Keeping counts for every address therefore
does not help a small shared cache; we did not isolate the cause. ARC and W-TinyLFU are the strongest online policies
up to \R{sz.256} vectors per table and up to \R{sz.1000} shared entries, but need ghost lists or a sketch and a window, whose hardware cost we did
not measure. The controller belongs to the LFU-reset family, so LFU-reset is the policy it should reach.

\subsection{The tie-break makes the controller lose to LRU}\label{sec:tie}
On the full traces the RTL-exact policy in mode 0 (the v2 rule) is below LRU at all four shared sizes simulated
from \R{sz.256} to \R{sz.100000} entries, at both lengths (Fig.~\ref{fig:tiebreak}): \R{p9.10M.256.v2}\% against
\R{p3b.10M.256.LRU}\% at \R{sz.256} entries and \R{p9.10M.100000.v2}\% against \R{p3b.10M.100000.LRU}\% at
\R{sz.100000} entries (\R{lab.10M} rows). At \R{sz.16} and \R{sz.64} entries, where LRU is low
(\R{p11.2M.16.LRU}\% and \R{p11.2M.64.LRU}\% at \R{lab.2M} rows), the v2 rule is above LRU (\R{p11.2M.16.v2}\% and
\R{p11.2M.64.v2}\%) but far below LFU-reset (\R{p11.2M.16.LFUr}\% and \R{p11.2M.64.LFUr}\%).

The cause is the tie-break. A new tag takes the slot of the victim, which was the lowest-indexed slot with the
minimum count, and starts at count one, so unless it is hit before the next miss it is again the lowest-indexed
minimum and is evicted next. On the row0 slice at \R{sz.256} entries, the v2 rule evicts the line installed by the
previous miss in \R{p9.prev.v2} of \R{p9.prev.v2.n} evictions; LFU-reset with oldest-first ties does so in
\R{p9.prev.old} of \R{p9.prev.old.n}. The ablation confirms it: LFU-reset with lowest-slot ties alone gives exactly
the hits of the RTL-exact policy, and LFU-reset with \R{rtl.cntw}-bit counters alone gives exactly the hits of
LFU-reset, at all four sizes and both lengths. The tie-break accounts for the whole gap (\R{p9.gap.10M.256} hits at
\R{sz.256} entries, \R{lab.10M} rows), saturation for \R{p9.sat} hits and their interaction for \R{p9.int}. Mode 1 is
no remedy: it beats LRU at \R{sz.256} entries but loses to it at \R{sz.1000} and \R{sz.10000} entries on both traces.

\subsection{Rotate-victim restores LFU-reset}\label{sec:fix}
We simulated three replacements for the lowest-slot rule: Rotate-victim, which starts the search for the minimum at
the slot after the previous victim; Rotate-step, whose pointer advances on every miss; and a young bit per line. At
depth \R{sz.256} the first two add \R{p9.ff.rs} flip-flops and the third \R{p9.ff.yb}. We implemented Rotate-victim
because it reproduces the policy the controller was written for: on the slices it gives exactly the hits of
oldest-first LFU-reset, and on \R{p9.probe.n} synthetic traces it differs from it on \R{p9.probe.rv}, against
\R{p9.probe.rs} for Rotate-step. Rotate-step got more hits than LFU-reset at \R{p9.rs.beats} of \R{p9.rs.pts}
full-trace points; we did not pursue it. In mode 0 Rotate-victim recovers \R{p9.rec}\% of the gap at every size, to one decimal,
and differs from oldest-first LFU-reset by \R{p9.rvdiff.lo} to \R{p9.rvdiff.hi} hits on the full traces; at
\R{sz.16}, \R{sz.64} and \R{sz.256} entries it equals LFU-reset exactly, in total and in every table. It beats LRU and
the v2 rule at all six sizes simulated, from \R{sz.16} to \R{sz.100000} entries (Fig.~\ref{fig:tiebreak}); at \R{sz.256} entries
and \R{lab.10M} rows it has \R{p11.10M.256.v3}\%, \R{d.v3lru} points above LRU and \R{d.v3v2} points above the v2
rule. In mode 1 all three candidates lose hits against the unchanged rule, so v3 changes mode 0 only.

The gain is not uniform. Inside the shared cache, \R{p11.tl.16} of \R{ds.tables} tables at \R{sz.16} entries,
\R{p11.tl.64} at \R{sz.64} and \R{p11.tl.256} at \R{sz.256} have fewer hits under v3 than under v2 (at \R{sz.16}
entries and \R{lab.2M} rows they lose \R{p11.lost.2M.16} hits while the total gains \R{p11.gain.2M.16}). On the
cold-start slices at depth \R{sz.16}, v3 has fewer hits than v2 (\R{p10.16.row0.v3} against \R{p10.16.row0.v2} and
\R{p10.16.row1M.v3} against \R{p10.16.row1M.v2} of \R{p9.slicereq} requests).

\section{Results: Hardware}\label{sec:res-hw}
\subsection{Verification}\label{sec:verif}
v3 passes the inherited tests at all three depths in both modes and the tie-order test in mode 0, and the \R{p10.mut} mutants of the
new rule (the v2 rule, \texttt{ptr} loading the victim instead of victim${}+1$, no wrap bit in Stage B) are caught.
The full mutation suite on v3 kills \R{p11.mut.tb} mutants with the testbench and \R{p11.mut.ls} more with the
lockstep check, and no non-equivalent mutant survives. In lockstep with the simulator, \R{p10.runs} RTL simulations
(three depths, both modes, two slices of \R{p9.slicereq} requests) show \R{p10.mism} mismatches, request by request;
at depth \R{sz.256} on the row0 slice v3 has \R{p10.256.row0.v3} hits where the v2 rule has \R{p10.256.row0.v2}.
Mode 1 is unchanged: its hit logs equal those of v2 line for line, and Yosys proves the v2 and v3 RTL equivalent in
mode 1 at depths \R{sz.16} and \R{sz.64} (\R{p11.eq.16} and \R{p11.eq.64} equivalence cells); the proof was not run
at depth \R{sz.256} and does not cover the netlists. At gate level, the \R{p11.gls.n} open-flow v3 runs (three depths, both modes, two clock targets, two slices) show
\R{p11.gls.mism} mismatching cycles against the RTL, and so do the commercial depth-\R{sz.256} netlists of both
versions before layout (\R{p13.mism}) and after routing at \R{p15.gls.period} ns (\R{p15.mism}); their hit logs equal the RTL logs. The SDF runs annotate
\R{p15.sdf.path}\% of the path delays but \R{p15.sdf.check}\% of the timing checks, so setup and hold rest on static
timing analysis (typical corner; other corners in Section~\ref{sec:corners}), not on simulation.

\textbf{Open flow.} On NanGate45 (pre-layout, one run per point) the Fmax of v3 in mode 0 differs from v2 by
\R{p11.nfmax.lo}\% to \R{p11.nfmax.hi}\%, inside the \R{p11.nnoise}\% change of the same-logic mode 1, so no Fmax cost
or gain of Rotate-victim is claimed from this flow.

\textbf{28nm.} With Genus at the typical corner (pre-layout, one run per point) the tightest met and loosest failed
periods are \R{p12.br.16.met} and \R{p12.br.16.fail} ns at depth \R{sz.16} for both versions and modes, and
\R{p12.br.256.met} and \R{p12.br.256.fail} ns at depth \R{sz.256} for all four version and mode pairs; at depth
\R{sz.64} they are \R{p12.br.64.met} and \R{p12.br.64.fail} ns except v3 in mode 1. The Fmax of a failing run
depends on its target period, so the bracket is the reliable figure. At a relaxed \R{p12.relax} ns, the area of v3
differs from v2 by \R{p12.a.16.m0}\%, \R{p12.a.64.m0}\% and \R{p12.a.256.m0}\% in mode 0 at the three depths, against
\R{p12.a.16.m1}\%, \R{p12.a.64.m1}\% and \R{p12.a.256.m1}\% in mode 1, whose logic is unchanged (proven at depths
\R{sz.16} and \R{sz.64}). Genus merges the per-group copies of the request address into one register; keeping all

\subsection{Corners}\label{sec:corners}
The layouts were optimised at the typical corner only, so failures at other corners are expected; Table~\ref{tab:hw}
and this section describe the existing layouts, not a sign-off. The typical-corner re-timing reproduces the
place-and-route reports for all five designs. At the slow corner every design fails setup, at depth \R{sz.256} on
register-to-register paths only (WNS \R{p16.ss.s.v2.10} and \R{p16.ss.s.v3.10} ns for the \R{p15.met} ns layouts of
v2 and v3). The derived slow-corner period, which ignores hold, is \R{p16.dp.v2.10} and \R{p16.dp.v3.10} ns for these layouts and
\R{p16.dp.v2.11} and \R{p16.dp.v3.11} ns for the \R{p15.gls.period} ns ones. Here v3 is ahead of v2 by
\R{d.dp10} ns, the opposite sign to the \R{p15.fail} ns typical-corner difference above, which suggests that both
differences lie within the variation of the flow; one layout per point cannot show this. The fast corner meets setup
for all designs.

Hold fails at the slow corner for the depth-\R{sz.256} layouts in the I/O path group only
(\R{p16.ss.hn.v2.10} and \R{p16.ss.hn.v3.10} paths for the \R{p15.met} ns layouts); register-to-register hold is
met (\R{p16.ss.hr.v2.10} and \R{p16.ss.hr.v3.10} ns). Path reports of the v3 \R{p15.met} ns layout explain this. The
constraints give inputs a delay of \R{p16.io.indelay} ns on the ideal clock edge; at the slow corner the data arrive
at \R{p16.io.arr} ns, while the capturing register's clock arrives at \R{p16.io.clk} ns through the propagated clock
tree, so the hold slack is \R{p16.io.slack} ns, and \R{p16.io.in2reg} of the \R{p16.io.all} failing paths start at an
input while \R{p16.io.r2o} end at an output. At the typical corner the saved database holds a clock source latency of \R{p16.io.lat} ns, written by the tool
during the flow, which cancels the \R{p16.io.tree} ns tree delay for these paths (slack \R{p16.io.ttslack} ns); the
added corners have none. At the fast corner hold fails in the I/O group of all four depth-\R{sz.256} layouts
(\R{p16.ff.hdn.lo} to \R{p16.ff.hdn.hi} paths) and, in the \R{p15.met} ns layouts only, on register-to-register
paths (\R{p16.ff.hrn.v2} and \R{p16.ff.hrn.v3} paths). Sign-off would need block-level I/O constraints with a clock latency
per corner, or multi-corner optimisation; neither was done.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:disc}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Corners and runs.} All layouts were optimised at the typical corner only, and every point is one run. The
bracket depends on the flow: earlier flows failed setup between \R{p15.fail} and \R{p15.oldfail} ns, and
\R{p15.met} ns needed two hold passes. The \R{p15.fail} ns difference between v2 and v3 is not resolved.
\item \emph{Timing in simulation.} The SDF simulations annotate no timing checks, so their zero violations are no
check; setup and hold rest on static timing analysis at the typical corner.
\item \emph{Energy.} Activity is simulated for \R{p15.cov.v2}\% to \R{p15.cov.v3}\% of the driver nets after routing,
with the bound stated above. Cycles per miss come from the testbench's fill handshake (\R{p13.fill.lo} to \R{p13.fill.hi} random cycles),
not from a memory system, and the two slices come from one dataset.
\item \emph{Scope.} One dataset, the first \R{lab.10M} of \R{ds.rows} rows; hardware built up to \R{sz.256} entries;
larger sizes are trace simulations of the RTL-exact model, up to \R{sz.100000}; the controller stores a \R{rtl.vecw}-bit word per line, not a full embedding vector.
\item \emph{Frequency.} At depth \R{sz.256} the pre-layout bracket (\R{p12.br.256.met} ns met) is tighter than the
post-route one (\R{p15.met} ns met), probably because the pre-layout runs have no wire load, parasitics or clock
tree; we did not separate these effects.
\item \emph{Power.} Registers and the clock tree take all but \R{p15.logic.lo}\% to \R{p15.logic.hi}\% of the
post-route power, so holding every line in flip-flops dominates the power. Clock gating, or storage in SRAM, is the
next step to try.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
In a hardware LFU controller for embedding lookups, the rule that breaks ties between equal counts decides whether
a shared cache of \R{sz.256} to \R{sz.100000} entries beats LRU on Criteo. Lowest-slot ties make the newest line
the next victim, and the exact model of the controller loses to LRU at all four sizes simulated in that range; a
single pointer (Rotate-victim) restores the hit rate of LFU-reset. In a 28nm FD-SOI flow through place and route,
the fix keeps the same met and failed clock periods and lowers energy per request by \R{p15.dE.row1M.abs} to
\R{p15.dE.row0.abs}\% in post-route simulation with a testbench fill model. Future work: a set-associative organisation, clock
gating, a realistic memory model behind the cache, body bias, multi-corner sign-off, more datasets, and hardware
counters on the H200.
