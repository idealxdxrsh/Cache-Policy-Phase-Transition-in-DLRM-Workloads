\begin{abstract}
Our LFU embedding cache finds the victim with a comparator tree that, like a priority
encoder, breaks ties by a fixed index order: the lowest index wins. With new lines starting at count one, this choice
decides whether the cache beats LRU. On Criteo, in one cache shared by all tables, its exact model
evicts the newest line almost every time on test slices and loses to LRU at every size simulated from \R{sz.256}
entries upwards; an ablation shows that the tie-break alone separates it from oldest-first LFU. The mechanism holds
for any counter-based cache that inserts at the minimum count with fixed-order ties. Rotate-victim starts the search
after the previous victim; for one pointer register it restores oldest-first LFU, raising the \R{sz.256}-entry hit
rate on the first \R{lab.10M} rows from \R{p9.10M.256.v2}\% to \R{p11.10M.256.v3}\%,
above LRU. With an STMicroelectronics 28nm FD-SOI standard-cell library (accessed through CMP) it keeps the original
clock period after routing at the typical corner and, in post-route simulation at a relaxed clock with a testbench
fill model, needs \R{p15.dE.row1M.abs} to \R{p15.dE.row0.abs}\% less energy per request. Code and aggregate
results are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
A deep-learning recommendation model (DLRM) maps each categorical feature of a request to a row of an embedding
table and combines the pooled rows with dense features in multilayer perceptrons~\cite{naumov2019dlrm}. Embedding
operations are memory-bound and can dominate inference time~\cite{gupta2020architectural,ke2020recnmp}, and their
accesses are skewed toward a small set of hot rows~\cite{adnan2022fae,agarwal2023bagpipe}. Systems therefore keep
hot rows in a faster memory, a DRAM cache in front of non-volatile memory~\cite{eisenman2019bandana} or GPU
memory~\cite{adnan2022fae,xie2022fleche,wei2022hps}, and each of these caches needs a replacement rule.

How well does a general-purpose cache serve these lookups? We profiled PyTorch's
\texttt{nn.EmbeddingBag}~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} on the Criteo stream on one H100 with Nsight Compute. The L2 read
hit rate of the embedding kernels falls from \R{p14.pt.64}\% to \R{p14.pt.16384}\% as the batch grows, with one
kernel per table (Fig.~\ref{fig:l2}); with all tables concatenated into one, the trend is the same.

Our trace simulator, given the shared-cache size closest to the L2's capacity in vectors, reaches
\R{p3b.2M.100000.LFUr}\% with LFU-reset over the first \R{lab.2M} rows. The comparison is indicative, not a
validation: the L2 has its own replacement policy, works on sectors smaller than a vector and also holds indices,
offsets and outputs, and the counters cover a window of batches that grows with the batch size, whereas the simulator
counts vector hits over the whole trace from a cold cache.

A frequency-aware cache next to the lookup datapath needs a small replacement unit. Our controller keeps a saturating
counter per line and finds the line with the smallest count with a tree of comparators. Without extra state such a
tree, like a priority encoder, breaks ties by a fixed index order; ours keeps the left operand unless the right one is
strictly smaller, so the lowest index wins. When the count of a new line restarts at one, the newest line holds the
smallest possible count and sits in the slot that won the last tie, so the next miss evicts it again unless it was hit
in between.

The argument needs three properties: new lines start at the minimum count, they take the victim's slot, and counts do
not decrease between misses. With any fixed tie order the newest line is then the next victim unless it is hit
first, so the mechanism applies to other counter-based caches built this way. How much it costs depends on the
workload, which we measured only on Criteo: there, in one shared cache of \R{sz.256} entries or more, the tie-break
decides whether our controller beats LRU. We explain this exactly and give a fix that costs one pointer register. In
detail:
\begin{itemize}
\item A trace-driven comparison of replacement policies on Criteo for per-table and shared caches, at two trace
lengths (Sections~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:res-policy}).
\item The finding that lowest-index ties make our controller (mode 0) lose to LRU in a shared cache at every size
simulated from \R{sz.256} to \R{sz.100000} entries, with an ablation showing that the tie-break alone separates it
from oldest-first LFU-reset (Section~\ref{sec:tie}).
\item Rotate-victim, a fix with one pointer register that reproduces LFU with oldest-first ties, verified request by
request against the simulator on two test slices in RTL simulation and cycle by cycle against the RTL at gate level
(Sections~\ref{sec:fix} and~\ref{sec:verif}).
\item Synthesis, place and route and multi-corner timing of both versions with a commercial 28nm FD-SOI library: at
the typical corner the fix meets and fails the same clock periods as the original design, before and after routing,
and in post-route simulation with a testbench fill model it needs \R{p15.dE.row1M.abs} to \R{p15.dE.row0.abs}\% less
energy per request (Section~\ref{sec:res-hw}).
\item Public RTL, simulators, flow scripts and aggregate results.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:bg}
\textbf{Embedding tables.} In the Criteo display-advertising data~\cite{criteo2014dac}, each of \R{ds.tables}
categorical columns holds hashed values; with one table per column, each distinct value is one embedding vector.

\textbf{Replacement policies.} LRU evicts the least recently used entry. LFU evicts the entry with the fewest
accesses; variants differ in what happens to a count on eviction. \emph{LFU-reset} discards the count and restarts
at one on re-insertion; \emph{LFU-persistent} keeps a count for every address ever seen, an idealised model of
history-keeping LFU. TinyLFU adds an admission filter that compares the estimated frequency of the new item with
that of the eviction candidate, and W-TinyLFU puts a small LRU window in front of the main
cache~\cite{einziger2017tinylfu}, estimating frequencies with a Count-Min sketch~\cite{cormode2005countmin}. ARC
balances a recency list and a frequency list using ghost lists of evicted keys~\cite{megiddo2003arc}. Belady's MIN
evicts the entry used furthest in the future and gives an offline upper bound for a cache that fetches on
demand~\cite{belady1966study}.

\textbf{Caches for recommendation.} Bandana stores embeddings in non-volatile memory behind a small DRAM
cache~\cite{eisenman2019bandana}, and FAE keeps the most accessed embeddings in GPU memory during
training~\cite{adnan2022fae}. ScratchPipe~\cite{kwon2022scratchpipe} and Bagpipe~\cite{agarwal2023bagpipe} look
ahead at future training batches to decide what to cache. For inference, Fleche uses one GPU cache for all embedding
tables~\cite{xie2022fleche}, the HugeCTR parameter server puts a GPU embedding cache in front of a storage
hierarchy~\cite{wei2022hps}, and EVStore layers several caches for embedding lookups~\cite{kurniawan2023evstore}.
UGache spreads one cache over several GPUs, for training and inference, with a policy based on a hotness
metric~\cite{song2023ugache}.
RecNMP moves embedding operations near memory~\cite{ke2020recnmp}. We study a lower level: the replacement decision
inside one small hardware cache, where the tie-break between equal counts is a property of the circuit.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Workload.} We replay the first \R{lab.2M} and \R{lab.10M} rows of the Criteo training file; nothing is
claimed for the rest of the file. Each categorical column is one table, and an empty field produces no access.

\textbf{Cache organisations.} \emph{Per-table} caches give each table its own cache of $C$ vectors, with $C$ from
\R{sz.p3min} to \R{sz.100000}. A \emph{shared} cache holds the vectors of all tables under the key (table, value), as
Fleche keeps one GPU cache for all tables~\cite{xie2022fleche}; the accesses of one row are issued in table order,
which is a modelling choice. Capacities are in vectors.

\textbf{Policies.} LRU, LFU-reset, LFU-persistent, TinyLFU with exact counters, W-TinyLFU with a Count-Min sketch
(both with a \R{p3.window}\% window), ARC and Belady; the shared-cache simulator runs all of them except TinyLFU and
Belady. In the simulator LFU-reset breaks count ties in favour of the entry installed earliest (\emph{oldest-first})
and saturates its counters at \R{rtl.simcap}. Unit tests and mutation tests check the simulators.

\textbf{Controller, v2.} The controller (tag \texttt{rtl-v2-final}, Fig.~\ref{fig:rtl}, parameters in
Table~\ref{tab:flows}) stores tags, data words, counters and valid bits in flip-flops, with no SRAM. Each line holds
a \R{rtl.vecw}-bit data word, not a full embedding vector: the vectors of Fig.~\ref{fig:l2} are \R{p14.vecbytes}
bytes, and storing them would need wider storage. The tag compare runs per group on registered copies of the request
address. On a miss, Stage~A (a minimum tree per group, registered in LOOKUP) and Stage~B (across groups, in EVICT)
find the line with the smallest key $\mathit{eff}$, which is zero for an invalid line and the count plus one
otherwise.

With \texttt{COUNTER\_MODE} 0 (the default) a new tag starts at count one, so the controller belongs to the LFU-reset
family; with mode 1 the tag continues from the count left in the slot. Both trees keep the left operand on equal
keys, so the victim is the \emph{lowest slot index} among the minimum counts.

\textbf{Controller, v3 (Rotate-victim).} v3 adds a pointer register \texttt{ptr} that loads victim${}+1$ (modulo the
depth) when a fill completes, and extends the tree key by one wrap bit, set when the slot index is below
\texttt{ptr}. The victim is then the first minimum-count slot at or after \texttt{ptr}, wrapping around. Tree depth,
counters and FSM are unchanged, and mode 1 keeps the v2 rule. The pointer adds \R{p11.ff.256} flip-flops at depth
\R{sz.256}.

\textbf{RTL-exact simulator.} To compare the controller with the policies on full traces, we wrote a trace-simulator
policy that models the RTL exactly: slots, lowest-index filling of invalid lines, the key $\mathit{eff}$, the
lowest-slot victim, the install count of each mode and counter saturation. It matches the RTL hit flag request by
request on two cold-start slices of \R{p9.slicereq} consecutive requests of the shared stream (\emph{row0} and
\emph{row1M}) at all three depths and in both modes (Table~\ref{tab:verif}). For the ablation we built LFU-reset with
lowest-slot ties, with \R{rtl.cntw}-bit counters, and with both; the last equals the RTL-exact policy in mode 0.

\textbf{Verification} uses a self-checking testbench (\R{rtl.tests} tests, plus a tie-order test for v3), mutation
testing, lockstep runs, Yosys equivalence checks and gate-level simulation (Table~\ref{tab:verif}).

\textbf{Implementation flows.} Table~\ref{tab:flows} lists the flows. The open flow maps the RTL to the NanGate45
library~\cite{nangate45} with Yosys and ABC~\cite{wolf_yosys,brayton2010abc} and runs OpenSTA~\cite{opensta} before
layout. The commercial flow uses an STMicroelectronics 28nm FD-SOI standard-cell library (accessed through CMP), with
Cadence Genus for synthesis and power, Xcelium for gate-level simulation and Innovus for place and route. The
floorplan has end caps, well taps and a four-net power grid: supply, ground and the two body-bias nets of FD-SOI,
which connect to the well taps. Genus states no area unit, so synthesis areas are in library units; Innovus reports
\umsq{}. Each design point was run once.

\textbf{Per-table and shared caches.} With per-table caches both LFU variants beat LRU at \R{sz.256} vectors
(Table~\ref{tab:hit}) and at every other capacity of the sweep, on both traces. In one shared cache the tables
compete for the same entries, and the two LFU variants part ways (Fig.~\ref{fig:shared}). LFU-reset beats LRU at
every shared size on both traces.

LFU-persistent does not. It falls below LRU at the smallest shared size on both traces and at the next size on the
longer one. At the three smaller sizes it also loses more than any other policy from the shorter to the longer trace,
\R{p3b.drop.lo} to \R{p3b.drop.hi} percentage points. Keeping a count for every address therefore does not
help a small shared cache; we did not isolate why.

ARC and W-TinyLFU are the strongest online policies up to \R{sz.256} vectors per table and \R{sz.1000} shared entries,
but they need ghost lists, or a sketch and a window, whose hardware cost we did not measure. The controller belongs
to the LFU-reset family, so LFU-reset is the policy it should reach.

\subsection{The tie-break makes the controller lose to LRU}\label{sec:tie}
On the full traces the RTL-exact policy in mode 0 (the v2 rule) is below LRU at all four shared sizes simulated from
\R{sz.256} to \R{sz.100000} entries, on both traces (Fig.~\ref{fig:tiebreak}; Table~\ref{tab:hit} gives the values
at the smallest of them). Below that range, at the two smallest sizes simulated, where LRU is low, the v2 rule is
above LRU but far below LFU-reset.

The cause is the tie-break, through the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:intro}. On the row0 slice at \R{sz.256}
entries, the v2 rule evicts the line installed by the previous miss in all but one of its \R{p9.prev.v2.n}
evictions; oldest-first LFU-reset never does.

The ablation confirms it. LFU-reset with lowest-slot ties alone gives exactly the hits of the RTL-exact policy, and
LFU-reset with \R{rtl.cntw}-bit counters alone gives exactly the hits of LFU-reset, at all four sizes and on both
traces. The tie-break therefore accounts for the whole gap to oldest-first LFU-reset (\R{p9.gap.10M.256} hits at \R{sz.256} entries on the
longer trace) and saturation for none of it. Mode 1 is no remedy: it beats LRU at the smallest of these sizes but
loses at the next two, on both traces.

\subsection{Rotate-victim restores LFU-reset}\label{sec:fix}
We simulated three replacements for the lowest-slot rule: Rotate-victim, which starts the search at the slot after
the previous victim; Rotate-step, whose pointer advances on every miss; and a young bit per line. The first two need
one pointer register, the third one flip-flop per line. We implemented Rotate-victim because it reproduces the policy
the controller was written for: on the slices it gives exactly the hits of oldest-first LFU-reset, and on
\R{p9.probe.n} synthetic traces it departs from it on \R{p9.probe.rv}, against \R{p9.probe.rs} for Rotate-step.
Rotate-step got more hits than LFU-reset at most full-trace points; we did not pursue it.

In mode 0, Rotate-victim recovers \R{p9.rec}\% of that gap at every size, to one decimal. On the full traces it
differs from oldest-first LFU-reset by at most \R{p9.rvdiff.hi.abs} hits, and at the three smallest sizes it equals
LFU-reset exactly, in total and in every table.

It beats LRU and the v2 rule at all six sizes simulated (Fig.~\ref{fig:tiebreak}). At the \R{sz.256}-entry size of
Table~\ref{tab:hit} it is \R{d.v3lru} points above LRU and \R{d.v3v2} above the v2 rule on the longer trace. In mode
1 all three candidates lose hits against the unchanged rule, so v3 changes mode 0 only.

The gain is not uniform. Inside the shared cache, \R{p11.tl.16} of the \R{ds.tables} tables have fewer hits under v3
than under v2 at the smallest size, and \R{p11.tl.256} at the size of Table~\ref{tab:hit}. On the cold-start slices,
the smallest design also has fewer hits with v3.

\subsection{Synthesis}
\textbf{Open flow.} On NanGate45 (pre-layout, one run per point) every Fmax change of v3 in mode 0 lies within the
\R{p11.nnoise}\% change seen between the two versions in mode 1, whose logic is unchanged, so no Fmax cost or gain
is claimed from this flow.

\textbf{28nm.} With Genus at the typical corner (pre-layout, one run per point), v2 and v3 meet and fail the same
clock periods in both modes (Table~\ref{tab:hw} for mode 0), and so do the smaller designs, except v3 in mode 1 at the middle
depth. The Fmax of a failing run depends on its target period, so the met and failed periods are the reliable figure.
At a relaxed clock, the area of v3 differs from v2 by at most \R{p12.a.16.m0.abs}\% in mode 0, while in mode 1, whose logic is
unchanged by construction (proven at the two smaller depths), the versions differ by up to \R{p12.a.256.m1.abs}\%.

Genus merges the per-group copies of the request address into one register. Keeping all of them (\R{p12.copies}
flip-flops) raises the Fmax of the failing runs by \R{p12.mo.v2}\% for v2 and \R{p12.mo.v3}\% for v3, one run each.

In those failing runs the option costs v3 \R{p12.mo.a.v3.abs}\% in area, more than any v2-to-v3 area difference in
mode 1, and v2 less. The merge-off netlists synthesised at \R{p13.period} ns, which go to gate-level simulation and
layout, cost v3 \R{p12.mo.a08.v3.abs}\% in area.

\subsection{Energy per request before layout}
For the merge-off netlists at \R{p13.period} ns (typical corner, pre-layout, no clock tree), with switching activity
from the SDF simulations, v3 needs \R{p13.dE.row1M.abs} to \R{p13.dE.row0.abs}\% less energy per request than v2 on
the two slices. Its power is slightly higher; the saving comes from fewer cycles, because with more hits fewer
requests wait for a fill (Table~\ref{tab:hw}).

Flip-flop outputs and ports are fully annotated with simulated activity, but only part of the combinational driver
nets are. Combinational logic takes at most \R{p13.logic.hi}\% of the power, and we take its share as the bound on
this error.

\subsection{Corners}\label{sec:corners}
The layouts were optimised at the typical corner only, so failures at other corners are expected; this section
describes the existing layouts, not a sign-off. Re-timing at the typical corner reproduces the place-and-route
reports. At the slow corner both versions fail setup on register-to-register paths, and the derived slow-corner
period, which ignores hold, is \R{p16.dp.v2.10} ns for v2 and \R{p16.dp.v3.10} ns for v3 (Table~\ref{tab:hw}).
Here v3 is ahead, the opposite sign to the typical-corner difference at the failing period, which suggests that both
differences lie within the variation of the flow; one layout per point cannot show this. The fast corner meets
setup.

Hold fails at the slow corner in the I/O path group only, while register-to-register hold is met. Path reports of v3
explain this: inputs arrive just after their delay of \R{p16.io.indelay} ns on the ideal clock edge, but the
capturing register's clock arrives at \R{p16.io.clk} ns through the propagated clock tree, so the hold slack is
\R{p16.io.slack} ns.

Of the failing slow-corner hold paths of v3, all but \R{d.in2reg.other} start at an input, and no register-to-output
path fails. At the typical corner
the saved database holds a clock source latency, written by the tool during the flow, that cancels the tree delay
for these paths; the added corners have none. At the fast corner hold fails in the I/O group and, in these layouts,
on some register-to-register paths (Table~\ref{tab:hw}). Sign-off would need block-level I/O constraints with a clock
latency per corner, or multi-corner optimisation; neither was done.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:disc}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Corners and runs.} All layouts were optimised at the typical corner only, and every point is one run. The
met and failed periods depend on the flow settings: other settings we tried failed at periods up to
\R{p15.oldfail} ns, and \R{p15.met} ns needed two hold passes.
\item \emph{Timing in simulation.} No timing checks are annotated in the SDF simulations; setup and hold rest on
static timing analysis.
\item \emph{Energy.} Activity is simulated for \R{p15.cov.v2} to \R{p15.cov.v3}\% of the driver nets after routing,
with the bound stated above. Cycles per miss come from the testbench's fill handshake, a random delay of up to
\R{p13.fill.max} cycles, not from a memory system, and the two slices come from one dataset.
\item \emph{Scope.} One dataset, the first \R{lab.10M} of \R{ds.rows} rows; hardware built up to \R{sz.256} entries,
larger sizes simulated with the RTL-exact model; lines hold data words, not full vectors.
\item \emph{Frequency.} The pre-layout runs meet \R{p12.br.256.met} ns and the routed layouts \R{p15.met} ns,
probably because the pre-layout runs have no wire load, parasitics or clock tree; we did not separate these effects.
\item \emph{Power.} Holding every line in flip-flops dominates the power (Section~\ref{sec:pnr}); clock gating, or
storage in SRAM, is the next step to try.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
Fixed-order ties, which comparator trees and priority encoders give without extra state, can decide whether a
counter-based LFU embedding cache beats LRU: with them, a new line that starts at count one becomes the next victim
unless it is hit first. On Criteo, in a cache shared by all tables, the exact model of our controller loses to LRU at
every size simulated from \R{sz.256} entries upwards, and one pointer register (Rotate-victim) restores LFU with
oldest-first ties. Through place and route in a 28nm FD-SOI flow, the fix keeps the clock period of the original
design at the typical corner and lowers energy per request by \R{p15.dE.row1M.abs} to \R{p15.dE.row0.abs}\% in
post-route simulation with a testbench fill model. Future work: a
set-associative organisation, clock gating, a realistic memory model behind the cache, body bias, multi-corner
sign-off, more datasets, and hardware counters on the H200.
